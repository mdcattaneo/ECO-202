## Solution 1. Population, sample, statistic, or distribution?

**Practice problem:** [Return to Problem 1](../#problem-1)  
**Class-guide connection:** [A statistic has a distribution](../../#c11-stop-1) and [One sample is one realization](../../#c11-stop-2)

### Question and strategy

Name the random mechanism before classifying its outputs. Keep the full fixed register separate from one selected sample, and keep a rule before selection separate from its one realized numerical value.

### Complete solution

The population is the fixed register of 500 invoice records. The observational unit is one invoice record, the variable is its dollar value, and the sampling mechanism consists of 16 independent uniform row selections with replacement. The statistic is the sample-mean rule $\overline X_{16}$.

The five objects classify as follows:

| Object | Classification |
|---|---|
| All 500 invoice values | Population distribution |
| The 16 selected invoice values | One realized sample distribution |
| $\overline X_{16}=16^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{16}X_i$&nbsp;before selection | Statistic viewed as a random variable |
| 84.50 dollars | One realized statistic |
| Distribution of the sample-mean statistic ($\overline X_{16}$) over possible ordered samples | Sampling distribution |

When the experiment is repeated, the selected rows and the new realized sample-mean value can change. The number 84.50 describes only the original realization. The rule $\overline X_{16}$, the 500-row distribution, its population mean, and the sampling distribution generated by that fixed rule remain unchanged.

The 16 observed values form only one sample distribution. The number 84.50 is only one output from the sample-mean rule. A sampling distribution instead assigns probabilities to all possible values of that rule across the repeated-sampling mechanism.

### Verification and limitation

The classification can be checked by asking whether the object exists before any random selection, is produced once by one sample, or summarizes the statistic over all possible samples. Nothing in this teaching mechanism establishes that the 500-invoice register represents invoices outside the company or time period that created it.

## Solution 2. Enumerate every possible sample mean

**Practice problem:** [Return to Problem 2](../#problem-2)  
**Class-guide connection:** [A statistic has a distribution](../../#c11-stop-1)

### Question and strategy

Because selection is ordered and with replacement, list both first-draw and second-draw possibilities. Build the sampling distribution from the resulting statistic values before calculating moments.

### Complete solution

The four equally likely ordered samples and means are

| Ordered sample | $(3,3)$ | $(3,9)$ | $(9,3)$ | $(9,9)$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Sample mean | 3 | 6 | 6 | 9 |

Therefore the sampling distribution is

| $\overline y_2$ | 3 | 6 | 9 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Probability | $1/4$ | $1/2$ | $1/4$ |

The probabilities are nonnegative and sum to one. Direct calculation gives

$$
\mathbb E[\overline Y_2]
=3\left(\frac14\right)+6\left(\frac12\right)+9\left(\frac14\right)
=6.
$$

The second moment is

$$
\mathbb E[\overline Y_2^2]
=9\left(\frac14\right)+36\left(\frac12\right)+81\left(\frac14\right)
=40.5,
$$

so

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\overline Y_2)=40.5-6^2=4.5=\frac92
$$

and

$$
\mathrm{SD}(\overline Y_2)=\sqrt{\frac92}=\frac{3}{\sqrt2}\approx2.1213
$$

in tens of dollars.

For one uniform population draw,

$$
\mu_Y=\frac{3+9}{2}=6
$$

and

$$
\sigma_Y^2
=\frac{(3-6)^2+(9-6)^2}{2}
=9.
$$

Thus $\mathbb E[\overline Y_2]=\mu_Y$ and $\mathrm{Var}(\overline Y_2)=9/2=\sigma_Y^2/2$.

If $(3,9)$ is observed, the realized sample distribution places one of its two observed values at 3 and one at 9, and its realized sample mean is 6. The population distribution, the complete three-value sampling distribution, its center, and its variance remain the quantities derived above.

### Verification and limitation

The direct enumeration and the independent-sum variance formula agree exactly. This calculation identifies the sampling distribution because all possible ordered samples were listed; a single observed pair would not do so.

### Nearby transfer

<details>
<summary>Reveal the transfer check</summary>

</details>

## Solution 3. Counts and proportions from indicators

**Practice problem:** [Return to Problem 3](../#problem-3)  
**Class-guide connection:** [Counts and proportions from indicators](../../#c11-stop-4)

### Question and strategy

Treat the count as a sum of zero-one indicators, then rescale the same random variable by $1/n$ for the sample proportion. Keep expectation and variance assumptions separate.

### Complete solution

For each indicator,

$$
\mathbb E[H_i]=p=0.36
$$

and

$$
\mathrm{Var}(H_i)=p(1-p)=(0.36)(0.64)=0.2304.
$$

Linearity of expectation gives

$$
\mathbb E[C_n]=np
$$

without requiring independence. Independence makes the covariance terms zero, so

$$
\mathrm{Var}(C_n)=np(1-p)
$$

and

$$
\mathrm{SD}(C_n)=\sqrt{np(1-p)}.
$$

Because $\widehat P_n=C_n/n$,

$$
\mathbb E[\widehat P_n]=p
$$

and

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\widehat P_n)
=\mathrm{SD}(\widehat P_n)
=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}.
$$

For $n=25$,

$$
\mathbb E[C_{25}]=25(0.36)=9,
\qquad
\mathrm{SD}(C_{25})=\sqrt{25(0.2304)}=2.4,
$$

and

$$
\mathbb E[\widehat P_{25}]=0.36,
\qquad
\mathrm{SE}(\widehat P_{25})=\sqrt{\frac{0.2304}{25}}=0.096.
$$

For $n=100$,

$$
\mathbb E[C_{100}]=36,
\qquad
\mathrm{SD}(C_{100})=\sqrt{100(0.2304)}=4.8,
$$

and

$$
\mathbb E[\widehat P_{100}]=0.36,
\qquad
\mathrm{SE}(\widehat P_{100})=\sqrt{\frac{0.2304}{100}}=0.048.
$$

A realized count is a sum of zeros and ones and must therefore be an integer. Its expectation is a probability-weighted center over possible counts and need not itself be a possible count.

When $n$ is multiplied by four, the count center $np$ is multiplied by four, the count standard deviation $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$ is multiplied by two, the proportion center $p$ is unchanged, and the proportion standard error is divided by two. Independence is used to add variances without covariance terms; linearity supplies both expectation formulas without requiring independence, provided the marginal indicator probabilities remain $p$.

### Verification and limitation

The proportion results also follow by dividing the count center by $n$ and count standard deviation by $n$. These are exact moment identities under the stated model; they do not by themselves specify a distributional shape.

### Nearby transfer

Before opening the check, use $p=0.25$ and $n=64$. Calculate the count center and SD and the proportion center and SE.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the transfer check</summary>

$\mathbb E[C_{64}]=16$, $\mathrm{SD}(C_{64})=\sqrt{12}\approx3.4641$, $\mathbb E[\widehat P_{64}]=0.25$, and $\mathrm{SE}(\widehat P_{64})=\sqrt{0.1875/64}\approx0.05413$.

</details>

## Solution 4. Square-root scaling in the fixed `wage1` population

**Practice problem:** [Return to Problem 4](../#problem-4)  
**Class-guide connection:** [Center and spread of a sample mean](../../#c11-stop-3) and [Counts and proportions from indicators](../../#c11-stop-4)

### Question and strategy

Hold the 526-row population and independent with-replacement mechanism fixed. Apply the exact sample-mean and sample-proportion formulas, then compare quantities that describe the full sampling distribution with the error from one realized sample.

### Complete solution

For every $n$, the exact sample-mean center is

$$
\mathbb E[\overline W_n]=\mu_W\approx5.896103.
$$

The standard errors are

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\overline W_{25})
=\frac{3.689574}{\sqrt{25}}
\approx0.737915,
$$

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\overline W_{100})
=\frac{3.689574}{\sqrt{100}}
\approx0.368957,
$$

and

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\overline W_{400})
=\frac{3.689574}{\sqrt{400}}
\approx0.184479.
$$

All are measured in 1976 dollars per hour because a standard deviation has the same units as its random variable.

The exact sample-proportion center is $p=197/526\approx0.3745247$ for every $n$. Its standard errors are

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\widehat P_{25})
=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{25}}
\approx0.096800,
$$

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\widehat P_{100})
=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{100}}
\approx0.048400,
$$

and

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\widehat P_{400})
=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{400}}
\approx0.024200.
$$

These standard errors are unitless because a proportion is unitless.

Each fourfold increase in $n$ doubles $\sqrt n$, so it divides $\sigma_W/\sqrt n$ and $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ by two. Dividing by four would confuse variance, which scales as $1/n$, with standard deviation, which is the square root of variance.

The realized error in the seeded sample is

$$
\overline w_{25}-\mu_W
=5.5708-5.896103
=-0.325303
$$

1976 dollars per hour. This one signed difference is not the standard error $0.737915$, which describes the standard deviation of $\overline W_{25}$ over all samples generated by the model.

Seeing 24 distinct rows among 25 draws means at least one row was selected more than once. Sampling with replacement explicitly permits that event. The draws remain independent because every draw again selects uniformly from all 526 rows.

### Verification and limitation

The ratios $0.737915/0.368957$ and $0.368957/0.184479$ are approximately two, as are the corresponding proportion-SE ratios. The calculation concerns repeated uniform selection from these fixed historical rows; increasing $n$ does not change which population is represented by the model.

### Nearby transfer

Before opening the check, calculate both standard errors for $n=64$ under the same fixed `wage1` model and state their units.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the transfer check</summary>

$\mathrm{SE}(\overline W_{64})=3.689574/8\approx0.461197$ in 1976 dollars per hour, and $\mathrm{SE}(\widehat P_{64})=\sqrt{p(1-p)/64}\approx0.060500$, which is unitless.

</details>

## Solution 5. Analytic benchmarks and simulation summaries

**Practice problem:** [Return to Problem 5](../#problem-5)  
**Class-guide connection:** [Simulation meets the analytic benchmark](../../#c11-stop-5)

### Question and strategy

Subtract the analytic benchmark from each empirical simulation summary, then identify which quantities belong to the probability model and which inherit Monte Carlo variation from a finite number of repetitions.

### Complete solution

The four differences are

| Statistic | $n$ | Simulation SD minus analytic SE |
|---|---:|---:|
| Sample mean | 25 | $0.745032-0.737915=0.007117$ |
| Sample mean | 400 | $0.183028-0.184479=-0.001451$ |
| Sample proportion | 25 | $0.097396-0.096800=0.000596$ |
| Sample proportion | 400 | $0.023938-0.024200=-0.000262$ |

The analytic SE is determined exactly by the fixed population and sampling mechanism. The simulation SD is the empirical standard deviation of 10,000 generated statistic values. It targets the analytic SE, but another finite simulation would generally produce a slightly different empirical SD because of Monte Carlo variation.

Increasing repetitions from 10,000 to 50,000 leaves the analytic SE, the within-repetition sample size $n$, the fixed population, and the sampling distribution unchanged. It would generally reduce the Monte Carlo discrepancy in the empirical summary.

A simulation checks whether an implementation behaves consistently with the analytic benchmark: large or systematic discrepancies may motivate inspection of code, inputs, or assumptions. Agreement is not a mathematical proof because the simulation observes only finitely many generated samples, while the analytic identity follows from the probability model.

The table does not establish that either sampling distribution has an exact Normal shape. It also does not show that the historical 526-row file represents current workers or any broader population.

### Verification and limitation

All four differences are small relative to the corresponding analytic SE, and they occur on both sides of zero. That pattern is compatible with finite simulation discrepancy, though code and design should still be verified independently.

## Solution 6. Audit one sampling-distribution report

**Practice problem:** [Return to Problem 6](../#problem-6)  
**Class-guide connection:** [Audit a sampling-distribution report](../../#c11-stop-6)

### Question and strategy

Audit the claim in the order object, calculation, interpretation, simulation, sampling mechanism, shape, and population scope. A correct numerical value does not rescue an incorrect description of what it measures.

### Complete solution

The first error is calling 5.5708 the sampling distribution. It is one realized value of the sample-mean statistic from one sample of size 25. The sampling distribution is the probability distribution of $\overline W_{25}$ over all samples generated by the fixed with-replacement design.

The analytic calculation for $n=400$ is

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\overline W_{400})
=\frac{3.689574}{\sqrt{400}}
=\frac{3.689574}{20}
\approx0.184479.
$$

This value is the standard deviation of the sample-mean sampling distribution. It is not a bound that every realized sample mean must obey and not the error in any one sample. A realized error is $\overline w_n-\mu_W$ and changes from sample to sample.

The simulation SD 0.183028 is an empirical summary from 10,000 repeated samples. Its difference from the analytic target is

$$
0.183028-0.184479=-0.001451.
$$

A finite simulation is not required to reproduce the analytic value exactly. The discrepancy alone therefore does not prove a problem with the probability model or code, although the implementation should always be checked.

Repeated rows are compatible with sampling with replacement because each selection again includes all 526 rows. A repeated row is a possible outcome, not evidence that replacement failed.

The reported center and spread do not establish an exact Normal shape. They also do not change the historical source or population coverage of the fixed file. Selecting 400 rows from the same 1976 file cannot by itself make that file describe current workers.

A six-sentence repair is:

> The number 5.5708 is one realized sample mean, while a sampling distribution describes the sample-mean statistic across repeated samples. Under the stated fixed-population model, the analytic standard error for $n=400$ is approximately 0.184479, which is sampling-distribution spread rather than a bound or one realized error. The simulation SD 0.183028 is close but not identical because a finite simulation has Monte Carlo variation. Duplicate rows are permitted under sampling with replacement. The displayed summaries do not establish an exact distributional shape. Increasing the sample size reduces sampling variability within the model but does not make the historical fixed file represent current workers.

For the final unaided transfer, the exact center is 50 and

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\overline X_{36})=\frac{12}{\sqrt{36}}=2.
$$

The realized error is $47-50=-3$. The simulation SD 2.06 is an empirical approximation to the analytic sampling-distribution SD 2. Neither the center-and-spread calculation nor the simulated SD establishes an exact shape or representation of a broader population.

### Verification and limitation

The analytic calculation, simulation subtraction, and definition of with-replacement sampling separately verify the repair. All conclusions remain conditional on the fixed historical population and stated independent uniform selection mechanism.
